A Fall.

"I was walking across a parking lot. A car was coming. I looked up — and tripped and fell on my face."

I'd been exercising for more than 45 years. Much of my adult life I was a runner, New York City and Twin City Marathons. About 10 years ago I cut back on running and started going to the gym. My routine was pretty straightforward. Every other day, I worked on 6-7 machines, gradually increased weight. Occasionally did some pushups. The gym I was at didn't allow outside trainers so I was figuring it out on my own. Before my fall I was seriously bored and moderately fit.

After the fall I stopped going to the gym, and stopped running completely. I was discouraged.

A friend suggested that a personal trainer might help and I found one at a local gym. We talked about my situation and he pointed out two very important points: while machines can be useful we live our lives in three dimensions, in movement. Machines only operate in two dimensions. We agreed to minimize machines. Second, he said that core work is an essential part of any program because it builds muscles that are a stabilization platform for movement. It was an excellent beginning to a new training program for me.

By accident — or maybe divine intervention! — I learned about sarcopenia. Had never heard of it before. It's the gradual, and almost universal decline in muscle and strength that happens as we age. And loss of strength leads to a loss of mobility, independence, and quality of life. This revelation set me on a brand new life journey.

I learned that no matter how old we are we can get stronger.

Sarcopenia.
Most people have never heard of it.

By the time we do, it's already been quietly happening for years. Things that used to be easy — stairs, grocery bags, getting up from the floor, standing up from a chair — have gradually become hard. So we stop doing things. And people start to see us as weak, frail, slow, fearful. And sadly, they're not wrong. BUT IT DOESN'T HAVE TO BE THAT WAY!

It begins in our 40s. Most people don't notice until their 60s or 70s — by then, years of muscle loss have quietly accumulated.
It accelerates dramatically after 60. The charts on the right should be a huge warning sign for everyone over 60. It's the point where resistance training can make a huge difference in how we live the rest of our lives.
Resistance training reverses it. Adults 65+ who train consistently see significant strength gains — regardless of how long they've been sedentary.
Most adults 65+ are not doing resistance training. They are paying a price they don't need to pay.

Sarcopenia is not inevitable.

01
We start with where you are now.
Every program starts with a thorough individual assessment. That includes your health history and your past experiences with exercising identifying your current capabilities and constraints. Then we conduct individual movement assessments such as sit-to-stand, standing on one leg, hand grip strength, and more. This will enable us to create a baseline that we will use to monitor progress.
02
Your Goals. Not Generic Ones.
People 65+ have much different exercise goals than people under 50. That is central to the FATM approach. We build around what you NEED to do, WANT to do, and USED to do but no longer can.
03
Train for Real Life.
We strength train for real life. Getting up from a chair. Preventing falls. Living independently. Mobility that lets us continue doing the activities we love. The metrics we use to plan your exercise program are the metrics of your daily life.
04
Progressive Overload.
As we exercise to get stronger we need to gradually and consistently increase the weight we push, pull, and lift — because over time as our bodies get stronger they will eventually adapt to the changes. We'll experience it as a "plateau." This is the key to resistance training and it's called progressive overloading.
05
Safety Is Non-Negotiable.
For adults 65+ an injury can derail everything. If someone 35 injures themselves during training, recovery can be brief. Training injuries for people 65+ are much more challenging. That's why the FATM embeds safety into every exercise strategy, every individual exercise routine, and every session. It's not an after-thought, it's the foundation of what we do.
06
Adjust. Reassess. Evolve.
Monitoring your progress is essential. Achieving your goals is why you're doing this and knowing where you are in relation to those goals tells us whether we're on the right track. We use quantitative and qualitative metrics — everything from number of exercise repetitions and weights used — to your ability to get out of a chair without using your arms.

Ready for more?

Excellent! Here are three more very important things you can do:

Increase your daily protein intake!
100 grams per day minimum, ideally 1 gram per pound of body weight — spread equally over breakfast, lunch, and dinner. Supplements are fine, avoid ones with heavy added sugar. Protein is the building block of muscle and it will help you as you begin exercising.
Start doing planks.
Another excellent way to build your core. There are dozens and dozens of examples of how to do a plank on YouTube — the most important point is to keep your back straight. The goal is to gradually increase the amount of time you hold it.
Add wall push-ups.
You can easily start with your hands high on the wall and as you get stronger, work your hands down. Also dozens of YouTube examples out there. A good starting place is doing 10 easy ones, rest for a minute, then do another 10, rest for a minute, and do another 10.

Safety & health
Is it safe for me to start exercising at my age, especially if I haven't worked out in years?

For the vast majority of people 65+, the answer is yes — and the research is unambiguous that the risk of not exercising is far greater than the risk of starting. That said, I take "starting where you are" seriously. We begin with a careful assessment, work at a pace that respects your body, and build up only as you're ready. Nothing about good training for our age group should feel reckless.

Do I need clearance from my doctor before we start?

If you have any cardiovascular condition, are recovering from surgery, or have a chronic condition your doctor is actively managing, yes — please talk to them first. For most other situations, a conversation with your doctor is a good idea but not required. I'll ask you a thorough health-history questionnaire either way, and I'd rather pause and check with your physician than guess.

I have arthritis (or osteoporosis, a hip replacement, balance issues, diabetes, a heart condition). Can you still work with me?

In most cases, yes. The Functional Aging Training Model I use was built specifically for people managing the realities of aging bodies — including the conditions you listed. Strength training is one of the best things you can do for arthritis, bone density, and blood-sugar control, and it's done a lot for my own joints. I'll need to know what you're dealing with so we can adapt the work, but a diagnosis isn't a reason to stop.

What happens if I get hurt or fall during a session?

I train one-on-one and I'm right there with you — spotting, cueing, and watching closely. Falls and injuries during sessions are extremely rare because the work is structured and supervised, but I carry liability insurance, I'm CPR/AED certified, and we have a plan for what to do if something does happen. Your safety isn't an afterthought. It's the whole point.

My qualifications & approach
What are your qualifications?

I'm an ISSA Certified Personal Trainer and an ISSA Certified Functional Aging Specialist — that second certification specifically covers the science and methods of training adults 65 and older. I also do continuing education through the Functional Aging Institute, who developed the training model I follow. I'm not a physical therapist, and I'm not a doctor — I'm a trainer who specializes in our age group.

Have you actually worked with people my age?

Yes. I started by working with friends in my own circle — people I trusted to give me honest feedback and tell me when something wasn't working. That's how I built my approach: real reps with real people in their 70s and 80s, not theory. As FA65Plus grows, I'm continuing to take on clients carefully and intentionally, so the work stays personal.

How is training someone my age different from training a younger person?

It's almost a different job. Younger trainers chase aesthetics, performance, and PRs. With clients 65+, the goals are different: keeping you out of assisted living, making the stairs feel easy again, helping you get up off the floor without thinking about it, keeping you steady on uneven ground. The exercises are adapted, the rest periods are different, the cueing is different, and the progression is much more gradual. I built FA65Plus around that difference.

Will you push me too hard? Or not hard enough?

This is the question I think about most. Too easy and nothing changes; too hard and you get hurt or quit. The honest answer is that I'll push you a little — strength gains require effort that feels real — but always with an exit ramp, and always while watching how your body is responding. We talk constantly during sessions. If something doesn't feel right, we change it. Period.

What sessions actually look like
What does a typical session involve? Will I be lifting weights?

A typical session runs about an hour: a thorough warm-up, then strength work (yes, including weights — appropriate ones), balance training, mobility, and a cool-down. The "weights" might be dumbbells, bands, kettlebells, or just your body — whatever fits where you are. We work in multiple planes of motion because real life happens in multiple planes of motion. You won't be on a leg-press machine staring at a TV.

Do I need to be able to get down on the floor?

No — but getting up and down from the floor safely is one of the most important skills for staying independent, so it's something we'll likely work toward over time, at your pace. If you can't do it now, we use chairs, benches, and standing variations. If you can, we'll keep that ability sharp.

How often should we meet, and where?

For most clients, twice a week is the sweet spot — enough frequency to build real strength, enough recovery between sessions. Some clients do well with once a week plus homework. Sessions can happen at a private training space, in your home, or — for some clients — over video. We'll figure out what fits your life.

Results & expectations
What can I realistically expect to feel in 1, 3, and 6 months?

One month: things start to feel a little easier — getting out of a chair, climbing stairs, carrying groceries. Most people sleep better, too.

Three months: measurable strength gains. You'll notice you don't hesitate before doing things you used to hesitate about.

Six months: a different relationship with your body. Better balance, more confidence on uneven ground, more energy in the rest of your day. None of this is hypothetical — it's the standard trajectory the research and my own clients consistently show.

I'm worried about losing my independence. Can training really help?

This is the central question, and the honest answer is yes — more than almost anything else available to you. The single biggest predictor of whether someone stays in their own home as they age is whether they can stand up from a chair without help. Strength is what protects independence. That's not a slogan; that's why I built FA65Plus.

What if I have a bad day and can't do much?

We adjust. Some days you'll show up tired, sore, or distracted, and we'll dial things back — maybe focus on mobility, balance, or just movement. Some days you'll surprise yourself. Both kinds of days are part of the process. The trainers who push through everything are the ones whose clients get hurt.

The Methodology

The Functional Aging Training Model

The FATM is a science-backed, individualized system designed specifically for adults 65+. It prioritizes independence, daily function, and quality of life — built around ten overarching goals, four cornerstones, two pillars, and seven key principles.

10 Goals of FATM

1
Maintain Independence in Daily Life
Preserve the ability to perform activities of daily living — standing up from a chair, carrying groceries, reaching overhead, climbing stairs, getting up from the floor.
2
Preserve and Build Strength
Maintain muscle mass (counteracting sarcopenia), improve functional strength especially in the legs, hips, core, and upper back — focused on everyday tasks, not maximal lifting.
3
Improve Balance and Fall Prevention
Enhance static and dynamic balance. Reduce fall risk — one of the biggest threats to healthy aging.
4
Maintain Mobility and Joint Health
Preserve range of motion in key joints (hips, shoulders, spine, ankles). Improve movement quality to reduce pain and stiffness.
5
Support Cardiovascular Health
Maintain heart and lung capacity for daily endurance. Improve work capacity for activities like walking, climbing, and household tasks.
6
Enhance Core Stability and Posture
Improve posture to reduce neck, shoulder, and low-back strain. Enable safer lifting, reaching, and twisting.
7
Reduce Injury and Pain Risk
Correct movement patterns, build tolerance to load gradually, and strengthen commonly vulnerable areas — hips, knees, shoulders.
8
Promote Longevity and Quality of Life
Extend healthspan, not just lifespan. Maintain confidence in movement.
9
Adaptability Across Ability Levels
Exercises are scalable for beginners and highly active adults. Strategies evolve as needs, health status, or limitations change.
10
Encourage Sustainable, Lifelong Activity
Emphasize habit formation, avoid burnout and overtraining, and support mental well-being and self-efficacy.

The Seven Key Principles of the Functional Aging Training Model

  • 📌  Assess, prioritize, and train all components of function.
  • 📌  Make purposeful training decisions.
  • 📌  Integrate multi-planar movement patterns.
  • 📌  Use isolation exercises only as supplementary elements.
  • 📌  Use seated exercises only when necessary.
  • 📌  Order sessions by energy and complexity.
  • 📌  Maximize safety through holistic awareness and sound teaching.

In the FATM approach, before participants begin lifting, pushing, pulling, or turning, we build a plan. Every step matters.

Conduct Individual Assessments
We conduct individual assessments that identify risks and limitations, set baselines for tracking performance, determine when referrals might be needed, and generate enthusiasm and confidence.

What the Research Shows

All About 80

By the time people reach 80, significant physical changes have typically occurred — but enormous variation exists. Genetics, lifelong activity, nutrition, and environment all play major roles. Here's what the research tells us.

💪 Muscle Mass & Strength
Sarcopenia is well-established by 80. Sedentary adults may have lost 30–40% of peak muscle mass. Strength losses can be even greater — up to 50% in some muscle groups. But significant gains are still possible with resistance training.
⚖️ Balance & Fall Risk
Falls become the leading cause of injury-related death. Reaction time slows. Vestibular function declines. Proprioception (knowing where your body is in space) becomes less reliable. Balance training directly addresses this.
🦴 Bone Density
Osteoporosis affects roughly 25% of women and 5% of men over 80. Hip fractures in this age group carry a 20–30% mortality rate within one year. Weight-bearing resistance exercise is one of the few interventions that rebuilds bone.
🧠 Cognitive Function
Processing speed, working memory, and executive function all decline. Exercise — particularly resistance and aerobic training — is one of the most evidence-based interventions for slowing cognitive decline.
💓 Cardiovascular Capacity
Maximum heart rate and VO2 max both decline. Daily activities that were effortless at 60 require more effort at 80. Cardiovascular training preserves the capacity needed for independent living.
🔄 Flexibility & Mobility
Connective tissue stiffens. Range of motion decreases in nearly every joint. Morning stiffness is common. Targeted mobility work can substantially improve quality of daily movement.
😴 Sleep & Recovery
Sleep architecture changes — less deep sleep, more frequent waking. Recovery from exercise takes longer. Training intensity and volume need to be calibrated accordingly — which is exactly what the FATM does.
🤝 Social & Psychological
Loss of independence is the number one fear among adults 80+. Social isolation increases. Exercise programs that build community and confidence address both the physical and psychological dimensions of aging.
